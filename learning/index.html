Session 01 · 1.1

Positioning before posting: the narrow wedge

  • ICP triangulation across three axes — industry × role × recurring operational pain — until it fits one sentence a stranger can repeat.
  • The expensive-problem test: the pain must be recurring, measurable, and cost more than $10k/year in labor, error, or lost revenue — or don't post about it.
  • Authority asymmetry: "AI consultant" loses every time to "I cut claims-intake time for mid-size P&C carriers." Pick a wedge you can defend in 10 minutes of technical questioning.
  • Profile as landing page: headline formula, banner as proof surface, Featured section as lead-magnet delivery, About section written as case narrative, not résumé.
  • Proof inventory: turning weak evidence — internal projects, unpaid builds, side experiments — into legitimate numbered claims without overstating them.
Speaker notes

The wedge must survive a technical cross-examination, not just sound good on a slide. "AI consultant" is a title; "I cut claims-intake time for mid-size P&C carriers" is a claim someone can test. The $10k/year threshold is a hard filter — anything under it produces content nobody buys from, because the reader can't justify spending to fix it.

Session 01 · 1.2

The content flywheel: five formats that convert

  • Teardown post — real before/after workflow with time and cost figures. Highest-converting format; draws inbound from operators, not peers.
  • Build log — screenshots of work in progress, including the failure. Builds technical credibility that survives a buyer's engineer.
  • Contrarian ops take — argues against tool worship and toward process. Drives reach and comment volume.
  • Result document post — multi-page PDF/carousel of a single engagement outcome. Drives profile views and saves.
  • Diagnostic checklist — reader scores themselves against it; doubles as the lead-magnet hook.
  • Cadence: 5 posts/week, allocated 3 authority / 1 proof / 1 offer-adjacent.
Speaker notes

The offer-adjacent post fails without the other four — it only converts because the first four built the trust it spends. Hook mechanics: the first 210 characters decide the open rate, line-break discipline keeps it scannable, never put a link in the post body (kills reach), and use the comment-as-CTA pattern instead of a link.

Comment harvesting: 10 strategic comments per day on ICP posts. The path comment → profile view → connect → conversation consistently outperforms cold connect requests.

Session 01 · 1.3

The outreach loop: a DM workflow that doesn't read as spam

  • Lead sources ranked by conversion: your own post engagers, commenters on ICP-adjacent posts, then a built list (Sales Navigator, Apollo, Clay) filtered by trigger events.
  • Four-touch sequence: Engage (meaningful comment, no ask) → Connect (no pitch) → Value drop (something useful and specific) → Soft ask (one sentence, one question).
  • Trigger-based openers: new role, funding, hiring posts, tech-stack signals, public complaints about a process — personalization from research, not flattery.
  • Qualification gate in the thread: owns the process? budget exists? timeline? — asked before the calendar link appears.
  • Reply triage, scripted by branch: interested, not now, wrong person, hostile — plus a no-response close that recovers roughly one in ten dead threads.
  • Volume ceilings and account safety: connection limits, message pacing — automated sending tools get accounts restricted.
Speaker notes

The qualification gate exists so the calendar only fills with people who can actually buy. The no-response close is a single message sent after the thread has gone cold — it quietly recovers about 10% of otherwise-dead conversations and should never be skipped.

Session 01 · 1.4

Lead magnet and booking mechanics

  • Build exactly one asset: the AI Readiness Teardown — a one-page diagnostic completed in four minutes, delivered as a PDF plus a 3-minute Loom.
  • Deliver in-thread rather than behind a gated form — trading email capture for conversation continuity is the better trade at this funnel volume.
  • Call-page copy: name the call "Automation Teardown," not "discovery" or "intro chat." Set a 25-minute cap and state what the prospect leaves with.
  • Show-rate operations: instant confirmation message, 24-hour reminder, and a three-question pre-call form that doubles as the first half of discovery.
  • Six funnel metrics worth tracking weekly: impressions → profile views → connects accepted → conversations started → calls booked → calls held.
Speaker notes

The six-metric funnel is diagnostic, not decorative: a weekly review of these six numbers in sequence tells you exactly which stage is actually broken, rather than guessing at "more content" or "better targeting."

Session 02 · 2.1 continued

Pricing math and productization

  • One-metric-moved rule: if you cannot name the single number the engagement moves, you cannot price it.
  • Value arithmetic: annualized value = labor hours saved × loaded rate + error cost avoided + revenue unlocked.
  • Price at 10–20% of year-one value; refuse anything below 3.5× your delivery cost.
  • Productize: fixed scope, fixed price, fixed timeline, plus a written change-order protocol — the three constraints that make delivery repeatable.
  • Paid diagnostic beats free audit: it converts at 60–80% into a build, proves budget exists, and the client pays you to produce the document you quote from.
Speaker notes

The 3.5× delivery-cost floor is a hard refusal line, not a negotiating opener — below it, margin erodes faster than volume can compensate.

Session 02 · 2.2

The TRACE discovery call: a call that prices itself

  • T — Trigger: why this, why now, what changed. No trigger means no deal, regardless of interest.
  • R — Reality: map the current process live on a shared screen, six steps maximum.
  • A — Arithmetic: volume × handling time × loaded rate, plus error and rework cost — stated out loud by the prospect.
  • C — Consequence: the twelve-month cost of doing nothing, in their own words and figures.
  • E — Entry: decision process, economic buyer, budget range, timeline, and what else is competing for the money.
  • Never quote on call one — the 48-hour architecture gap buys the room to price correctly.
Speaker notes

Forty verbatim quantification questions and a range-anchor script for asking about budget without flinching or apologizing are provided in SOP 2.3. Multi-stakeholder mapping matters here: economic buyer, technical blocker, end-user champion — identify which one is actually on the call. When asked "just ballpark it for me," the answer is still: send the architecture in 48 hours, not a number today.

Session 02 · 2.3

Objection handling: the enterprise eight

1“We can build this in-house.”
2“ChatGPT already does this for twenty dollars a month.”
3“That's more than we expected to spend.”
4“Legal and security will never approve sending our data out.”
5“What happens when it hallucinates?”
6“Our data isn't clean enough yet.”
7“Just send me a proposal.” (the brush-off)
8“I need to run it past the team.”

Each gets the same treatment: the fear underneath it, the reframe script, and the specific proof asset you deploy.

Speaker notes

Risk-reversal structures that cost nothing to offer: pilot gate, milestone billing, a written accuracy SLA, and client-owned IP. Disqualification: four red flags predict non-payment, and there is a specific script for ending a call early without burning the relationship — use it rather than chasing a bad-fit deal.

Session 02 · 2.4

Proposals and the closing presentation

  • One-page proposal: their words, their numbers, three options, one explicit recommendation — anything longer reads as padding.
  • Closing call: present the architecture and the arithmetic, then the price. Then the silence after the number — and what happens when you fill it.
  • Good / better / best framing with the middle option deliberately engineered to win, and decoy pricing done honestly.
  • Terms that protect margin: 50% up front, net-0 on milestones, written scope boundary, kill fee, client-delay clause, auto-renewing maintenance.
  • Post-proposal follow-up: five touches over eleven days, then a close-the-loop message that gets a decision either way.
Speaker notes

Do not speak first after stating the price — the instinct to fill silence with a discount is the single most common self-inflicted margin loss in this call.

Session 03 · 3.1 continued

Orchestration choices and guardrails

  • Choose orchestration honestly: n8n when the client needs to see and touch the workflow, application code when anything is stateful or regulated, Temporal when long-running retries and durability actually matter — picking the heaviest option by default is how projects die.
  • Idempotency, retry policy, and dead-letter queues — three mechanics behind most client-visible incidents.
  • A per-engagement eval harness: a 30-case golden set built before go-live, with the accuracy threshold written into the SOW as the acceptance gate.
  • Secrets, tenancy, and data boundaries: what you host, what stays in the client's infrastructure, and what never leaves it — this conversation is what Session 04 sells against.
Speaker notes

Flag explicitly that the data-boundary conversation here is the seed of Session 04's entire local-deployment offer — students should start noting which clients flinch at this question.

Session 03 · 3.2

Onboarding protocol: the first ten days

  • Ninety-minute kickoff agenda plus an access checklist naming who grants what by which date — access delays are the single largest cause of blown timelines.
  • Process discovery workshop: as-is map → to-be map → written acceptance criteria, signed before a line of code is written.
  • Communication contract: one shared channel, a standing 20-minute weekly call, and a written Friday update — no status meeting without a working screen.
  • The scope wall: what is included, what becomes a change order, who signs change orders, and what they cost.
  • Risk register and the client-caused-delay clause that moves your deadline when their access, data, or decisions slip.
Speaker notes

The "no status meeting without a working screen" rule is a discipline check as much as a client-management tactic — it forces momentum rather than narration.

Local LLMs & Infrastructure Cost Control

Self-hosting open-weight models to cut token spend, and packaging local deployment as a high-margin offer for buyers whose compliance team already blocked the cloud.

Speaker notes

This session re-prices Session 03's delivery cost from the infrastructure up — it only works because students enter it with real billing data and a real shipped system from Session 03.

Session 04 · Objective & target outcome

Stand up a working local inference stack

  • Prove its economics against your own billing data instead of a vendor's benchmark.
  • Route traffic hybrid so cheap work stops hitting frontier pricing.
  • Turn the whole capability into a three-tier offer aimed at compliance-constrained buyers.
Target outcome
  • A deployed stack serving one real Session 03 workload
  • Published accuracy and cost delta against your cloud baseline
  • A completed TCO model naming your breakeven token volume

Pre-session preparation in full. Hardware, either path works: a machine with 16GB+ VRAM (RTX 4090/5090, A6000) or Apple Silicon with 32GB+ unified memory; or a cloud GPU account (RunPod, Lambda, Vast.ai) with at least $25 in credit. Nobody is blocked by hardware.

Speaker notes

LiteLLM's "avoid it when: never" is deliberate — it is the seam that keeps client application code unchanged regardless of which model sits behind it, so it belongs in every stack.

Session 04 · 4.1 continued

Model choice and sizing

  • Model selection by job, not by leaderboard: small instruct models for classification, extraction, and routing; mid-size for drafting; frontier cloud for genuinely hard reasoning. The goal is a router, not a replacement.
  • Quantization tradeoffs across fp16, 8-bit, and 4-bit, measured on your eval set rather than assumed.
  • VRAM sizing arithmetic: parameters × bytes-per-parameter + KV cache + overhead, feeding the model-to-card matrix.
  • Context and KV-cache math, batching, and why throughput collapses at long context if you size for parameters alone.
Speaker notes

Push students away from benchmark worship here — the only quantization decision that matters is the one validated on their own golden eval set, not a published leaderboard number.

Session 04 · 4.2

The economics: build the model, not the narrative

  • Honest token accounting: cost per million in and out, cache-hit rates, retries, and the agent fan-out multiplier that quietly makes real usage 3–8× your estimate.
  • Self-hosting TCO in full: GPU capex or hourly rate, power, cooling, redundancy, idle time, and your own operations hours.
  • Breakeven analysis across owned GPU, hourly cloud GPU, and frontier API, as a function of monthly token volume and utilization rate.
  • Where local wins: high-volume classification and extraction, PII- or PHI-bound workloads, predictable always-on batch processing, offline and air-gapped environments.
  • Where local loses: spiky low volume, frontier-level reasoning, very small teams, and anything needing state-of-the-art code generation.
  • Hybrid routing: cascade cheap to expensive behind confidence gates, instrumented to report measured savings, not projected ones.
Speaker notes

Saying "where local loses" out loud in front of a buyer is what makes the rest of the pitch credible — a self-hosting pitch that omits the operations-hours line of the TCO is the one students must not write.

Session 04 · 4.3

Local RAG and agents for enterprise

  • Stack: Postgres with pgvector or Qdrant, a local embedding model, metadata-aware chunking, and a reranker.
  • Permissions-aware retrieval — the requirement that kills most enterprise pilots after the demo. Document-level ACLs enforced at query time, not filtered after.
  • Agent patterns on smaller local models: tool-calling reliability, enforced structured output, guardrails, and confidence-triggered fallback to a cloud model.
  • Evaluation and drift: run the same Session 03 golden set against local and cloud, then publish the delta to the client rather than hiding it.
  • Deployment shapes: on-premise box, the client's VPC, or your managed GPU — each with its own backups, model updates, monitoring, and GPU-level alerting.
Speaker notes

Permissions-aware retrieval is the line item most pilots skip and the one that kills enterprise deals post-demo — enforce ACLs at query time, never as a post-filter.

Session 04 · 4.4

Productizing privacy and cost control as an offer

  • Two buying triggers, and they are not technical: compliance pressure (PHI, PII, HIPAA, GDPR, data residency, no-training clauses) and a runaway API bill someone in finance has started asking about.
  • Offer construction: Local AI Assessment at $5k–10k → Deployment at $25k–75k → Managed Hosting at $4k–15k/month.
  • Savings-share pitch usually loses to fixed fee plus retainer — attribution disputes, measurement arguments, capped upside.
  • Surviving security review: data-flow diagram, model provenance, dependency inventory, and a no-egress attestation you can actually defend.
  • Who to sell to: healthcare, legal, financial services, defense-adjacent manufacturing, EU data residency cases, and any firm whose CISO has already blocked public chat tools.
Speaker notes

The three-tier offer mirrors Session 02's ladder deliberately — same architecture, re-priced around infrastructure and compliance instead of workflow automation.
